From course completion to business impact.

01 · What is worth solving?

Course completion was visible. Business value was not.

A successful learning program should improve what people can do, not only whether they finished a course. I reframed the question around an outcome the business cared about: whether participation and consistency in sales training were connected to vehicle sales performance.

02 · What could be dramatically better?

Evaluate learning as a behavior-change system.

The opportunity was to move from reporting activity to understanding impact. Instead of treating every completion equally, the analysis could examine how much people trained, whether they returned consistently, how performance changed over time, and whether the pattern held across different levels of experience.

03 · What should we bet on first?

Test the sales-performance signal before expanding the claim.

I started with measures the team could interpret and act on: training participation, consistency over time, and vehicle sales performance. Segmenting by tenure helped test whether the relationship was limited to newer employees or appeared across experience groups.

04 · How did we make it real?

Build rigor into the analysis without overstating causality.

I established performance baselines, compared sales before and after training, examined results across training cycles and tenure groups, and separated completing more training from engaging consistently over time. The work produced a meaningful business signal while preserving an important distinction: observational data alone does not prove that training caused the full difference.

05 · What changed?

A strong signal opened a broader annual impact question.

Employees who trained more, and trained more consistently, also demonstrated stronger sales performance across tenure groups. At the scale of Hyundai’s dealer network, even a modest per-person difference could represent meaningful commercial impact.

The current annual analysis now brings together training participation, sales trends, and customer-experience verbatims to understand what learners engaged with, what customers experienced, and where the next year’s training strategy should evolve.

  • Evaluate impact across the full year rather than isolated training cycles.
  • Connect learning behavior with sales-performance trends.
  • Analyze customer-experience verbatims for evidence of behavioral change.
  • Turn the findings into recommendations for the next year’s program strategy.
