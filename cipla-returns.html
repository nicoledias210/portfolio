01 · Find the stakes

Start with the decisions behind the report.

Returns information came from multiple reports and required manual consolidation. Finance needed to understand the financial exposure; Supply Chain needed to investigate the patterns behind it. I started with their business questions and aligned the definitions of return value, quantity, and reporting dates.

How much?

View dollars alongside units so financial exposure and volume can both inform priorities.

What is driving it?

Separate expiry, damage, shipping issues, overstock, and other return reasons.

Where do we look?

Narrow the concern to customers, medications, and underlying return records.

The goal was a dependable view that users could explore during reviews. Summary metrics needed a clear path to the records behind them. One actual use involved an increase in return value: Finance traced it to expiry-related concentration in products and customers, and Supply Chain investigated inventory and ordering patterns.

03 · Choose the bet

A useful first version starts with numbers people trust.

I prioritized a reconciled view and the breakdowns needed for recurring reviews. The first version helped users identify where to investigate; more advanced analysis could follow once the core reporting was dependable.

Include first

The investigation essentials

  • Shared metric definitions and a defined reporting window.
  • Return value and quantity, with reason, customer, and product breakdowns.
  • Basic trends using valid dates and drill-downs to records.
  • Scheduled refresh and basic error monitoring for operational use.
Defer until ready

Depth with dependencies

  • Detailed carrier analysis until shipping questions and data coverage justified it.
  • Return rates until compatible sales or shipment denominators were available.
  • Extensive historical comparisons until dates were dependable.
  • Forecasting and advanced anomaly detection until the foundation was stable.

Prototype and pipeline work moved in parallel.

Business + dashboard

Early Power BI mock-ups → stakeholder feedback → refined filters, groupings, and investigation paths.

Engineering + data

Pipeline setup → discrepancy investigation → corrections and reconciliation.

I did not need to wait for the production pipeline to learn whether the experience answered users’ questions. The prototype clarified the experience while validation established trust in the production data.

04 · Make it real

The hardest work happened beneath the charts.

Initial reports contained substantial discrepancies. I worked back and forth with Engineering and business stakeholders to reconcile totals, investigate outliers and test records, and validate incoming pipeline data. I also coordinated with data governance to standardize missing, ambiguous, and inconsistent return reasons at the source.

A real data-quality decision

When historical records said “1969.”

Transferred records without associated dates appeared as 1969. A date cutoff alone could have hidden records that belonged in scope, so we addressed both date quality and the historical reporting window.

1969?

A displayed date was not sufficient evidence of when a return occurred.

Investigate the source and recover authoritative dates where possible.
Supported date recovered

Correct with traceability and classify within the agreed reporting window.

Date remains unknown

Keep outside dated comparisons; account for the records separately where needed for reconciliation and disclose coverage.

Validate the corrected, undated, and out-of-window populations without double counting.

Keep it running

Scheduled refresh removed routine manual rebuilding. Incremental refresh reduced the need to reload the full history, while alerts supported investigation of errors.

Keep delivery moving

During the project manager’s absence, I maintained the issue tracker, set priorities, coordinated progress reviews, and followed outstanding issues.

Make the handoff count

I prepared a handoff document, captured lessons learned, and arranged follow-up meetings after launch.

05 · Measure the outcome

A reporting product that became part of the workflow.

Finance used the dashboard in recurring discussions and monthly leadership reviews. Finance and Supply Chain could investigate high-value return patterns through reasons, products, customers, and individual records. After handover, refreshes continued without my routine manual involvement; alerts supported exception handling.

$4M+

Returns covered by the reporting product. This is business scope, not savings generated.

Operational use

Recurring business reviews and a confirmed expiry-related investigation.

Internal recognition

The project received a Cipla award for process improvement.

How I would measure it more precisely today

The next measurement plan would track preparation time for recurring reports, time to answer investigation questions, and follow-up actions informed by the dashboard. Refresh reliability and reconciliation discrepancies would serve as operational guardrails. These are proposed measures, not recorded results.

What I learned

A useful analytics product connects business definitions, reliable data, and a clear investigation path. I learned to coordinate those pieces together and plan for ownership after launch, as well as delivery itself.

Outcomes are described through confirmed use, automation, and internal recognition. No quantified savings or reduction in returns is attributed to the dashboard.
